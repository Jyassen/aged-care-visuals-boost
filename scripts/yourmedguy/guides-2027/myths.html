02Myth 1

"Medicare is too complicated to understand."

The myth

Only experts can make sense of Medicare, so I'll just pick whatever the ad says.

The reality

Medicare has lots of details, but the decisions you make come down to four parts and two roads. Learn that map and the rest is just checking your doctors and your drugs.

The four parts

PartWhat it coversWho runs it
Part AHospital stays, skilled nursing, hospiceMedicare
Part BDoctors, outpatient care, labs, preventive careMedicare
Part CMedicare Advantage: a private plan that replaces how you get A + B, usually with drugs built inPrivate companies
Part DPrescription drugsPrivate companies

The two roads

Road 1

Original Medicare + Supplement + Part D

Any doctor in the U.S. who takes Medicare. Higher monthly premiums, low and predictable costs when you're sick. Dental, vision and hearing are not included.

Road 2

Medicare Advantage

A network (HMO or PPO) in your area. Often a $0 premium, copays as you go, a yearly out-of-pocket maximum, and extras like dental and vision with limits.

A quick way to think about it: Road 1 costs more each month but less when you're sick. Road 2 often costs less each month, but you pay more as you use care, up to the plan's yearly limit.

New York bonus: you can buy a supplement any time of year, with no health questions, as long as you have Parts A and B. Most states don't allow that, so New Yorkers have more freedom to change roads later.

03Myth 2

"All Medicare plans are basically the same."

The myth

It's all government Medicare, so the plan I pick doesn't really matter.

The reality

Medicare Advantage plans are built by private companies, and they differ by hundreds of dollars a year in premiums, copays, drug costs and yearly limits. Two plans with the same name can even differ by county.

What varies on 2027 New York plans

What variesTypical 2027 range in New York
Monthly premium$0 on many plans, up to about $100 to $200 on plans with richer benefits
Specialist copayAbout $35 to $55 a visit. Primary care is usually $0.
Hospital stayOften $300 to $500 a day for the first 4 to 7 days, then $0
Yearly out-of-pocket maximumAbout $7,000 to $9,850 for in-network care
Drug deductible$0 on generic tiers for most plans; up to $700 on brand-name tiers
NetworkHMO (referrals, in-network only) or PPO (more freedom, higher costs out of network)
ExtrasDental, vision, hearing, over-the-counter cards and gym benefits, each with its own limits

How to compare two plans in ten minutes

1

Write down your doctors and your drugs. Name, dose, pharmacy. This is the single most useful thing you can bring to any comparison.

2

Check the network by office address, not just the doctor's name. Call the office and ask what they take for 2027.

3

Price your drugs on each plan. The Plan Finder at medicare.gov does this for free, and so do I.

4

Look at the worst case, not just the premium. What would a hospital stay cost you on each plan?

What I see every fall: a $0 premium plan and a $50 premium plan can be the same company. The difference is usually the specialist copay, the hospital copay and the dental limit. Pick based on how you use care, not the premium.

04Myth 3

"Medicare is free."

The myth

I paid into Medicare my whole working life, so everything is covered at no cost once I turn 65.

The reality

Part A is usually premium-free, but Part B has a monthly premium for nearly everyone, and you still pay deductibles and a share of costs. Help is available if your income is limited, and most people who qualify never apply.

05Myth 4

"Original Medicare covers everything."

The myth

Once I have Parts A and B, I don't need anything else.

The reality

Original Medicare is good coverage, and you can see any doctor in the country who takes Medicare. But it has two big holes: no yearly spending cap, and no coverage for most prescriptions, routine dental, vision or hearing aids. That's why almost everyone adds something on top.

What's covered, and what isn't

ServiceOriginal Medicare alone
Hospital staysCovered, after the Part A deductible
Doctor visits and outpatient careCovered at about 80% after the Part B deductible. The other 20% is yours, with no yearly limit.
Preventive careCovered, usually at $0
Prescription drugsNot covered. You need Part D or a Medicare Advantage plan with drugs built in.
Routine dental, vision, hearing aidsNot covered
Long-term custodial careNot covered

Two ways to fill the gaps

A supplement (Medigap) + Part D

Plan G covers everything Original Medicare leaves behind except the Part B deductible. Plan N has a lower premium with small copays. In New York, your age doesn't set the price, and you can apply any time of year.

A Medicare Advantage plan

Puts a yearly out-of-pocket maximum on your care, usually includes drugs, and often adds dental, vision and hearing with limits. You use the plan's network.

Don't skip drug coverage because you take nothing today. Go 63 days or more without Part D or other creditable drug coverage after you're first eligible, and a late penalty is added to your premium for as long as you have Part D.

06Myth 5

"I'm still working, so I can ignore Medicare."

The myth

My job's insurance is fine. I'll deal with Medicare when I retire.

The reality

Sometimes that's exactly right. Whether you can wait depends on the size of the employer providing your coverage, and the mistakes here come with lifetime penalties. Check before you decide.

Can you delay Part B?

20 or more employees

The job's plan usually pays first. Many people delay Part B without penalty and sign up when the job coverage ends. You get up to 8 months after the job or the coverage ends, whichever comes first.

Under 20 employees

Medicare usually pays first. If you don't sign up for Part B, your employer plan may pay very little. Check with your benefits office, in writing.

The COBRA trap. COBRA and retiree coverage don't count as active job coverage for Medicare. If you leave your job and go on COBRA instead of signing up for Part B, your 8-month window keeps running. Miss it and you can face a lifetime penalty and a gap in coverage. If you're 65 or older and losing job coverage, sign up for Part B before or as your job coverage ends.

07Myth 6

"I need a broker, or I'll make a terrible mistake."

The myth

Medicare is a trap unless someone sells me the right plan.

The reality

Plenty of people choose well on their own with free resources. A good broker earns a place at your table by doing the comparison work for you and by still picking up the phone after you enroll. Either way, you should know what the free help is.

When a broker helps most

1

You take several medications or see specialists, and you want every plan priced against your actual drugs and doctors.

2

You have Medicare and Medicaid, or a chronic condition, and want to know if a Special Needs Plan fits.

3

You're leaving a job after 65 and want the Part B paperwork done right the first time.

4

You want one person to call when a claim, a bill or a drug refill goes sideways.

What a licensed, independent broker costs you

Nothing. Brokers are paid by the insurance company you enroll with, at rates set by Medicare, so the plan costs you the same whether you use one or not. An independent broker works with several companies and can compare them side by side. Ask any broker which companies they represent. If they only sell one, that's not a comparison.

08From me to you

What I'd tell my own parents

If my own parents sat down at my kitchen table with their Medicare mail, this is what I'd tell them.

1

Don't pick a plan because of a TV ad or a gift card offer. Pick it because your doctors, your drugs and your budget line up.

2

Write down every medication, dose and pharmacy before you shop. It's the single most useful thing you can bring.

3

Call your doctors' offices and ask which plans they take. Websites are often out of date.

4

Look at the worst case, not just the premium. What would a hospital stay or a cancer diagnosis cost you on this plan?

5

Check if you qualify for help. Extra Help, Medicare Savings Programs and EPIC can save thousands a year.

6

Review your coverage every fall. Plans change every January, even if you don't.

7

Don't go it alone. Ask someone who does this every day, and who will still pick up the phone after you enroll.
